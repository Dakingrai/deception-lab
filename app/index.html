Map 4 · How deception datasets are made

Deception probes are trained on one dataset and judged on others. The out-of-distribution claim is only as meaningful as the differences between those datasets, and those differences are rarely stated. Datasets are usually described by scenario (insider trading, sandbagging, a kept secret). What plausibly matters to a probe is how the dishonesty was produced: whether the model was told to lie, given a goal that deception serves, cast as a character, pressured by the user, or trained to hide something. Each route leaves different traces in the input. A probe can learn the trace instead of the lie, for example by picking up an instruction to deceive that sits in the prompt.

This map sorts lie and deception data by how it was built. It covers dedicated benchmarks and also papers on lying, deception, and truth or belief probing that synthesize their own data, since much of what we know about probes comes from the latter. A dataset is included if its authors treat its outputs as lies or deception. Data where the dishonest speaker is a character the model plays (persona, role-play, fiction) is included but marked as contested. Whether those outputs are the model saying something it does not believe is an open question, discussed separately.

The map has four lenses, shown as tabs. The first groups datasets by what pushes the model to lie: an instruction or request, a situational incentive, pressure from the user, speaking in character, training, or nothing at all. The other three group the same datasets by whose voice is lying, who is meant to be misled, and where the cue to be dishonest sits. Each group opens into the construction routes inside it, and each route into its datasets, each with a verified dishonest and honest example. A line joins two bubbles when the same paper built datasets in both. The map has two purposes. The first is to make construction explicit, so that "generalises out of distribution" can be read as "transfers from construction A to construction B." The second is to find gaps: routes to dishonesty that no dataset covers, or covers only in a form where the cue is visible in the input. These gaps are candidates for new evaluation sets. They are not conclusions about what models do.
